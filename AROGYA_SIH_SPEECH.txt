 2. THE PROBLEM — WITH NUMBERS THEY CANNOT ARGUE WITH
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
KEYWORDS:  26 percent · 90 million · 70 percent of out-of-pocket · 6 to 10 percent

⏱ 0:50–2:30

  "Four numbers. This audience will recognise all of them.

   ONE — ADHERENCE.
   India has roughly 90 MILLION adults living with type 2 diabetes.
   Hypertension prevalence is around 35%.
   And the National NCD Monitoring Survey found that only 26% of rural
   patients with diabetes take their prescribed oral medication REGULARLY.
   Meta-analyses put anti-diabetic adherence at about 43%.

   Read that again. We are not failing to DIAGNOSE these people.
   We are not failing to PRESCRIBE for them.
   We diagnose them, we prescribe correctly — and then more than half the
   therapeutic value evaporates between the counter and the kitchen shelf.

   A prescription that is not taken is not medicine. It is paper.

   TWO — COST.
   Medicines account for roughly 70% of out-of-pocket health spending in
   India — about 60% of outpatient out-of-pocket cost.
   Out-of-pocket expenditure ROSE to 43.4% of total health spending in
   2022–23.
   Meanwhile a strip of metformin can cost ₹45 branded and ₹14 at a Jan
   Aushadhi outlet. Same molecule. Same strength.
   The information gap is not clinical. It is COMMERCIAL.

   THREE — SAFETY.
   And this one is yours. Studies estimate only 6–10% of adverse drug
   reactions in India are ever reported. Some estimates of under-reporting
   through spontaneous channels run as high as 98%.
   Of the ADRs that DO get reported — physicians file 64%.
   PHARMACISTS FILE 15%.

   Fifteen percent. From the profession that handles the medicine LAST,
   sees the patient MOST OFTEN, and is trained specifically to catch this.

   That is not a competence gap. That is a TOOLING gap.

   FOUR — LANGUAGE AND LITERACY.
   A prescription in English. A patient who reads Kannada. A dosing
   instruction — 'BD', 'HS', 'SOS' — that is effectively a professional
   cipher. We hand people a coded document in a second language and then
   call them non-compliant when they get it wrong."

  ⚠ DO NOT round these numbers up. If asked for a source, name it:
    National NCD Monitoring Survey 2017–18; ICMR-INDIAB 2023;
    Indian Journal of Pharmacology on ADR under-reporting; PvPI ICSR analysis.

 4. WHAT WE BUILT — DEMO NARRATIVE
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
KEYWORDS:  photograph · patient confirms · five checks · nothing auto-confirms

⏱ 3:30–5:30

  "Let me walk you through one patient's journey. This is BUILT and WORKING,
   not a mockup.

   STEP 1 — THE PRESCRIPTION.
   The patient photographs their prescription. Printed text goes through
   local OCR. If the handwriting defeats it — and doctors' handwriting
   often does — it falls through to a vision AI model.
   Measured on a rotated, blurred, noisy sample: conventional OCR scored
   ZERO. The vision model recovered all three medicines at 98% confidence.

   STEP 2 — AND THIS IS THE MOST IMPORTANT DESIGN DECISION WE MADE.
   Nothing is ever auto-confirmed. Ever.
   The patient reviews every extracted field before anything is saved.
   Because a misread DOSAGE is not a UX bug — it is a patient safety
   incident. We built the human checkpoint in as a hard gate, not a
   courtesy.

   STEP 3 — THE SAFETY SCREEN. Five checks run automatically:
     1. BANNED DRUGS — against CDSCO prohibitions. Nimesulide above
        100mg, ranitidine, banned fixed-dose combinations.
     2. ALLERGY CROSS-REFERENCE — and this is the Augmentin case.
        The patient wrote 'penicillin'. The prescription says 'Augmentin'.
        No shared text. We catch it via DRUG CLASS mapping.
     3. CONDITION CONTRAINDICATIONS — metformin against recorded kidney
        disease, for example.
     4. PREGNANCY — a hard red flag. Warfarin, doxycycline, ACE inhibitors.
     5. DUPLICATE THERAPY AND INTERACTIONS — two NSAIDs on one script.

   STEP 4 — PRICE, ON THE SAME SCREEN.
   Augmentin ₹205 branded, ₹68 generic. Sixty-seven percent.
   Deliberately AFTER the safety information. Money should never be the
   first thing a patient weighs against a safety warning.

   STEP 5 — ADHERENCE.
   Reminders, and a dashboard that measures adherence the way clinicians
   do — proportion of days covered, against the 80% threshold, because
   below 80% a chronic medicine largely stops delivering its benefit.
   Not a vanity score. A clinical one.

   STEP 6 — THE EMERGENCY QR CODE.
   A scannable code. No login — because a paramedic cannot be asked to
   authenticate. Blood group, allergies, conditions, current medicines,
   surgeries, who to call.
   And it auto-derives red alerts: 'ON BLOOD THINNERS — bleeding risk.'
   'ON INSULIN — consider hypoglycaemia if unconscious.'
   The patient never typed those. We inferred them from their drug classes.

   Plus: a chatbot grounded in a curated drug database with citations,
   eight Indian languages, and a community health noticeboard."

  ⚠ If asked "is this live?" — be precise:
    BUILT AND WORKING: OCR, safety screening, price comparison, reminders,
    emergency QR, chatbot, multilingual UI, adherence dashboard.
    NOT YET: real SMS (needs DLT registration), doctor interface,
    hospital network. Say so plainly.

 6. THE ROADMAP — DOCTOR INTERFACE
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
KEYWORDS:  patient-entered has a ceiling · clinician-verified · two-tier trust

⏱ 6:45–7:20

  "Now the honest limitation of everything I have described.

   Every piece of data in Arogya today is PATIENT-ENTERED. Patient-entered
   data has a ceiling. A patient may write 'sugar problem' where a clinician
   would write 'type 2 diabetes mellitus, diagnosed 2015, on metformin
   1000mg BD.'

   So: a DOCTOR INTERFACE.
   A verified clinician, with the patient's consent, can view and correct
   the record. Fix a misread drug name. Confirm a diagnosis. Add what the
   patient did not know to mention.

   This creates TWO-TIER TRUST inside the record:
     ▸ PATIENT-REPORTED — useful, but flagged as such
     ▸ CLINICIAN-VERIFIED — carries clinical weight

   And critically — a full AUDIT TRAIL. Who changed what, when.
   Because the moment a doctor can edit a medical record, provenance stops
   being a nice-to-have and becomes a legal requirement.

   For this audience there is a second version worth naming:
   a PHARMACIST INTERFACE. The professional who actually dispenses,
   verifying what was dispensed and flagging the ADR at the counter —
   feeding the 85% of pharmacovigilance data India currently loses."

 7. THE ROADMAP — HOSPITAL NETWORK
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
KEYWORDS:  islands · real-world evidence · what India actually takes ·
           consent first

⏱ 7:20–8:10

  "The final ambition, and the largest one.

   Today every hospital is an ISLAND. Your prescription history at Hospital A
   is invisible at Hospital B. The patient is the only thread connecting
   them — and the patient forgets.

   We want to bring hospitals onto a shared, CONSENTED layer. Two payoffs:

   FOR THE PATIENT — continuity. Your allergy is known before the second
   hospital prescribes. That is the Augmentin story, prevented.

   FOR PUBLIC HEALTH — and this is the part that should excite the
   researchers in this room:
   AGGREGATED, DE-IDENTIFIED REAL-WORLD EVIDENCE.

   Not what a trial in another country showed.
   What Indian patients ACTUALLY take, ACTUALLY tolerate, ACTUALLY afford,
   and ACTUALLY stop taking — and WHY.

   Which molecules get abandoned in month two.
   Which combinations produce ADRs that never reach PvPI.
   Which districts are paying brand prices where a generic exists.

   India is called the PHARMACY OF THE WORLD. We manufacture medicine for
   the planet. But we have remarkably thin real-world evidence on how our
   OWN population uses it.

   ⚠ AND I WILL SAY THE HARD PART BEFORE YOU ASK IT.
   This is the most dangerous idea in the entire pitch.
   Aggregated health data is exactly what the DPDP Act 2025 exists to

     1. EXPLICIT, PURPOSE-SPECIFIC CONSENT — revocable, in the patient's
        own language
     2. DE-IDENTIFICATION BEFORE AGGREGATION — always
     3. PATIENT BENEFIT FIRST, RESEARCH SECOND — never the reverse
     4. NO SALE OF PATIENT DATA. Ever. If that is the revenue model,

 9. Q&A BANK — CRITICAL AND INTUITIVE QUESTIONS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
GOLDEN RULE: If you do not know, say "I don't know — here's how we'd find out."
Judges reward calibrated honesty and punish bluffing. Every single time.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 A. SAFETY AND CLINICAL RISK  (expect the hardest questions here)
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Q1. "What if your OCR misreads a drug name and the patient takes the wrong
     medicine?"
A.  The strongest question in the room, and it drove our core architecture.
    Three defences:
    (a) NOTHING AUTO-CONFIRMS. The patient reviews every field. There is no
        code path that saves a medicine without human confirmation.
    (b) Low-confidence and handwritten scans are FLAGGED for extra care.
    (c) Extracted names are FUZZY-MATCHED to a curated drug database, so
        'Paracetmol' resolves to Paracetamol rather than becoming a new drug.
    We treat OCR output as a DRAFT, never as a fact.

Q2. "Your drug database is small. What if a medicine isn't in it and you
     miss a dangerous interaction?"
A.  Correct, and we say so IN THE UI — 'no flags found' explicitly states it
    is not a guarantee of safety, because our database is a limited sample.
    We also added a second pass that scans the RAW prescription text for
    banned drugs even when the medicine isn't in our database — we found
    that gap in testing when nimesulide was silently dropped.
    Production requires a licensed drug database. That is a procurement
    line item, not an engineering problem.

Q3. "Isn't the pregnancy red flag dangerous? What if she stops a medicine
     she genuinely needs?"
A.  This is why our wording is precise. We never say 'stop.' We say:
    'Do not take this before speaking to your doctor.'
    The flag is a PROMPT TO ASK, not an instruction to discontinue.
    We accept a higher false-alarm rate here deliberately — the asymmetry
    is stark. A false alarm costs a phone call. A missed teratogen can cost
    a lifetime. That asymmetry justifies the conservatism.

Q4. "Your chatbot uses an LLM. What about hallucination?"
A.  Three layers:
    (a) RETRIEVAL-GROUNDED — it answers from a curated, citable drug
        knowledge base, not free generation.
    (b) EVERY CLAIM CARRIES A CITATION the patient can inspect.
    (c) EMERGENCY LANGUAGE SHORT-CIRCUITS THE MODEL ENTIRELY. Chest pain,
        overdose, suicidal ideation — the LLM is never called; it routes
        straight to 'get help now.'
    And if the model is unavailable, it degrades to deterministic template
    answers rather than failing or improvising.

Q5. "Who is liable if a patient is harmed?"
A.  Answer this honestly — do not claim immunity.
    Arogya is positioned as an INFORMATION AND ADHERENCE tool, not a
    diagnostic or prescribing device. It never diagnoses, never changes a
    dose, never tells anyone to stop a medicine. Every medical output
    carries a disclaimer and routes to a clinician.
    That said: liability, terms of service, and whether this ever crosses
    into medical-device territory require formal legal review BEFORE real
    patient data is collected. We have not done that yet. It is Phase 0.

Q6. "Do you need CDSCO approval? Is this a medical device?"
A.  Software that only stores, displays and reminds is generally not a
    regulated medical device. Software that makes a CLINICAL RECOMMENDATION
    can be. We have deliberately stayed on the safe side of that line — we
    surface information and route to a clinician; we do not recommend
    therapy. But I will not pretend to be the authority on that. It needs a
    regulatory opinion.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 B. COMPETITION AND DIFFERENTIATION
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Q7. "1mg already does reminders. Why would anyone use yours?"
A.  Their reminder is a REFILL PROMPT. Ours is an ADHERENCE INTERVENTION.
    The difference shows up in one place: we will tell you the cheaper
    generic exists. They structurally cannot lead with that — it cannibalises
    their own margin.
    Also: no commerce platform does allergy cross-reference, pregnancy
    contraindication, or CDSCO banned-drug screening at prescription review.

Q8. "What stops 1mg from building this tomorrow? They have more money."
A.  Technically, nothing. I won't pretend we have a technology moat.
    But they cannot copy the INCENTIVE. The moment a commerce platform
    prominently steers patients to the ₹9 generic, it damages its own
    economics. Our credibility on price comes from not selling medicine —
    and that is not a feature they can ship.
    Our defensible asset is the CONSENTED LONGITUDINAL RECORD and the trust
    required to hold it.

Q9. "Isn't ABDM going to make you redundant?"
A.  The opposite. ABDM is infrastructure — identity and record exchange.
    It is not a patient-facing adherence, counselling or safety-screening
    application. 450+ solutions have integrated with it precisely because
    it is a platform, not a product. We intend to be one of them. ABDM
    solves the plumbing problem so we don't have to.

Q10. "Why should a pharmacy college care about a software project?"
A.  Because it is a research and teaching instrument.
     ▸ A live adherence dataset for pharmacy practice research
     ▸ A pharmacovigilance capture point where India currently loses ~90%
       of signal
     ▸ A student training environment for counselling and DUR
     And because it makes your profession's contribution measurable. You
     cannot argue for a larger clinical role for pharmacists using data
     that was never captured.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 C. PRIVACY, DATA AND ETHICS  (expect faculty to press hardest here)
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Q11. "Your emergency QR is public. Isn't that a massive privacy hole?"
A.  It is a deliberate, stated trade-off, and I'll defend it plainly.
     A paramedic cannot log in. Authentication would defeat the feature at
     the exact moment it matters.
     Mitigations: a 32-byte random token that cannot be guessed or
     enumerated; the patient chooses what goes on it; and a REGENERATE
     button that instantly invalidates every printed card.
     What it does NOT protect against is someone photographing the QR.
     We say that in the UI before the patient prints one. Informed
     trade-off, not a hidden risk.

Q12. "How does the hospital data-sharing plan comply with DPDP 2025?"
A.  Consent must be explicit, purpose-specific, in the patient's language,
     and revocable. De-identification happens BEFORE aggregation.
     Retention and erasure have to be implemented in code, not just written
     in a policy document.
     And we will not sell patient data. If we did, every trust argument in
     this pitch collapses.

Q13. "You're sending prescription images to Google's API for OCR. Is that
      compliant?"
A.  Sharp question, and the honest answer is: it is a conscious trade-off
     that needs revisiting before production. Local OCR runs first and
     handles most printed prescriptions with nothing leaving the device's
     server. Only images it cannot read fall through to the vision model.
     For production, options are an India-region processing agreement, an
     on-premise model, or making cloud OCR opt-in per upload. Proposed data
     localisation rules point the same direction.

Q14. "What if the patient enters wrong data — wrong allergy, wrong condition?"
A.  Then the safety screen inherits that error, and we should be honest
     that garbage in produces garbage out. Two mitigations:
     (a) The DOCTOR INTERFACE — clinician-verified data outranks
         patient-reported data.
     (b) We never present flags as verdicts. Everything resolves to
         'confirm with your doctor,' which is exactly the behaviour that
         catches a wrong entry.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 D. ADOPTION, BUSINESS AND SCALE
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Q15. "Your users are often elderly or low-literacy. Will they use an app?"
A.  This shaped the design more than any other constraint.
     ▸ PHONE OTP login — no password to forget
     ▸ EIGHT INDIAN LANGUAGES
     ▸ SMS FALLBACK — the reminder must work when the app is closed, which
       is most of the time
     ▸ CAREGIVER ACCESS on the roadmap, because for many elderly patients
       the real user is an adult child
     And an honest caveat: we have not yet usability-tested with elderly
     low-literacy users. Until we do, that claim is a design intention, not
     a validated result.

Q16. "How do you make money without selling medicine?"
A.  Deliberately unsolved, and I'd rather say that than invent a number.
     Directions that do NOT break the trust model:
     ▸ B2B licensing to hospitals and insurers for adherence outcomes
     ▸ Government / public-health deployment
     ▸ De-identified, consented research access under strict governance
     What we will not do: pharmacy commissions or advertising. Both
     reintroduce exactly the conflict of interest that is our whole
     differentiation.

Q17. "Why would a doctor bother using your interface? They're overloaded."
A.  They wouldn't, for altruism. The value has to be selfish:
     a patient arriving with a STRUCTURED, VERIFIED medication history and
     adherence record saves consultation time immediately.
     The pitch to a doctor is not 'help us' — it is 'stop spending the first
     five minutes reconstructing what your patient is taking.'

Q18. "How do you get hospitals to share data? They see it as an asset."
A.  Honestly, this is the hardest problem in the roadmap — harder than any
     engineering in it. Realistic sequence:
     ▸ Start where incentives already align — public hospitals, insurers
       who benefit from adherence, NABH accreditation requirements
     ▸ Ride ABDM, which already gives hospitals a compliance reason
     ▸ Give before taking: hospitals get analytics on their own population
       first
     This is a partnerships and policy problem wearing a technology costume.

Q19. "What have you actually validated? What's still a claim?"
A.  Answer precisely. VALIDATED: OCR accuracy on printed and degraded
     images; vision fallback recovering medicines conventional OCR scored
     zero on; safety screening catching a penicillin-to-Augmentin class
     match; price comparison; reminder catch-up after downtime.
     NOT VALIDATED: real-world adherence improvement; clinical review of
     our drug content; usability with the elderly; anything about the
     hospital network. Those need a pilot with real patients.

Q20. "What's your biggest weakness?"
A.  Ask this of yourself before a judge does. Best answer:
     "The drug knowledge base. Everything clinical rests on it, and ours is
     a curated sample, not a licensed clinical database. Until it is
     licensed and clinician-reviewed, our safety screening is a working
     demonstration of a mechanism — not a clinical-grade tool. I would
     rather say that here than have a judge discover it."

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 E. CURVEBALLS
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Q21. "Why not just use SMS? Everyone has SMS."
A.  We do — SMS is the fallback layer. But SMS is one-way. It cannot show a
     prescription review screen, run a safety screen, display a price
     comparison, or log adherence. SMS is a REMINDER CHANNEL, not a care
     platform. Use it for reach, not for the product.

Q22. "What happens if the internet is down?"
A.  Reminders are scheduled server-side and catch up when the app
     reconnects — we specifically fixed exact-minute matching so a missed
     window doesn't lose a dose permanently. Offline-first local storage is
     a genuine gap and it is on the roadmap.

Q23. "Isn't this just a to-do list with medical branding?"
A.  A to-do list doesn't know that Augmentin is a penicillin. It doesn't
     know nimesulide above 100mg is restricted in India, or that the same
     molecule costs ₹9 instead of ₹34. The reminder is the DELIVERY
     MECHANISM. The clinical reasoning underneath is the product.

Q24. "You're students. Why should we believe you can execute this?"
A.  You shouldn't — on the basis of ambition. Judge us on what is already

     The hospital network needs partners and policy work we cannot do
     alone. That's precisely why we are presenting it to a room that
     contains those partners.
